# Structural Limits: What the Architecture Makes Possible and Impossible

## What It Makes Structurally Possible

### 1. Intent-Driven Automation
A human writes a one-page Flight Plan in `missions/`. The `launch-mission` skill reads it and fans out into Kanban cards with explicit parent-child dependencies, budgets, and verification requirements. The human never writes a task card. The human writes intent. The system decomposes.

### 2. Persistent Multi-Session Execution
Unlike ephemeral chat sessions that die when context compresses, a mission spans sessions, restarts, and tool boundaries. A builder works a card for three hours, crashes, and the dispatcher reclaims it. A reviewer picks up its gated child the next day. Every card carries the same `mission_id`, and the board is the durable record connecting them.

### 3. Observable Agency Costs
Every card a mission runs generates rows in `task_runs`: tokens consumed, turns used, latency, verification outcomes. The After-Action Review is not a separate bookkeeping chore. It is a view of the database, generated by `debrief-mission` after execution. The cost of delegation is measurable, mission by mission.

### 4. Composability Without Lock-In
Because the leaf execution is goal text in a standard Kanban card body — judged by `goal_mode` against acceptance criteria — any tool that speaks `/goal` can join a pipeline. We do not ask other tools to adopt a mission format. The Flight Plan compiles down to primitives they already understand.

### 5. Human-in-the-Loop Governance
The "blocked" state in Kanban is the operationalization of residual control. The Flight Plan's Constraints section declares the human gates; `launch-mission` builds them into the cards; the Kanban system enforces them. A worker cannot proceed past a gate without a human unblock. Intent is preserved because control is preserved.

## What It Makes Structurally Impossible

### 1. Opaque Black Boxes
You cannot launch a mission without seeing its decomposition. `launch-mission` presents the card graph — who does what, in what order, gated where — and runs pre-flight Go/No-Go checks before anything executes. Ambiguity is caught at the keyboard, not in production.

### 2. Unbounded Delegation
The Flight Plan's budget constraints (tokens, time, turns) become card budgets like `goal_max_turns`. A runaway agent exhausts its budget and the card blocks for human review. No silent exits, no unlimited spend.

### 3. Single-Tool Lock-In
Because leaf execution is the standard `/goal` pattern, we cannot be trapped in a single vendor's ecosystem. If a worker tool changes, we swap the profile. The Flight Plan does not change. The Kanban runtime does not change.

### 4. Fire-and-Forget Automation
The Kanban runtime's durability means every card has history, retry logic, and human intervention points. You cannot "launch and ignore." The system expects supervision, and the Flight Plan makes that expectation explicit in its pause conditions and human gates.

## The Architecture

```
Human writes Flight Plan (missions/)
    → launch-mission runs Go/No-Go checks, decomposes into Kanban cards (kanban_create)
    → Dispatcher claims cards, spawns workers (configured profiles)
    → Workers execute goal text; goal_mode judges against acceptance criteria
    → Structured handoff (kanban_complete with metadata)
    → debrief-mission reads task_runs → After-Action Review
    → Human reviews via dashboard, /kanban commands, or the AAR
```

This is a shift from "I ask an AI to do something" to "I define a mission, and the system orchestrates execution across specialized agents, preserving my intent and giving me control."

## The missions.md Synthesis

`missions.md` is the public face of this system. It is the brand that says: mission-driven project management is how humans direct agent ecosystems. The site explains the philosophy, shares Flight Plan templates, and shows live execution.

The Hermes Kanban system is the runtime that makes it real. The Flight Plan is the page that makes it intentional. The `/goal` primitive is the standard that makes it universal.
